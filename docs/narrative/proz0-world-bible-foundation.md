# 1. CORE PREMISE

## 1.1 Civilization recovery, not mere survival

**PO DECISION**

Humanity’s mission is not only to survive one hostile or unfamiliar planet. The broader framing is Civilization Recovery: preserve enough human continuity to establish a durable future.

**CONFIRMED**

The player fantasy is visible construction of safety, capability, infrastructure, mapped knowledge, and settlement growth. The planet mystery exists alongside this rebuilding story rather than replacing it.

**PROPOSAL**

The strongest narrative framing is to treat the Seed Colony as both an inheritance and a question.

It carries:
- knowledge that deserves preservation;
- systems and assumptions inherited from the old civilization;
- cultural memory that may be incomplete, idealized, contested, or context-poor;
- the responsibility to decide what should be rebuilt faithfully and what should be changed.

The mission therefore has two long arcs that continually mirror one another:

1. **Rebuild humanity.**
2. **Understand what another civilization built before humanity arrived.**

The prior civilization should not function primarily as an external lore object. It should become a mirror that makes humanity’s rebuilding choices more meaningful.

## 1.2 The central dramatic tension

**PO DECISION**

The governing thematic question is:

> When given the chance to build civilization again from the beginning, should humanity restore the old civilization or build something better?

**PROPOSAL**

This should not be reduced to “old = bad” or “new = good.”

A mature form of the theme is:

> Which parts of a civilization are knowledge worth preserving, which are habits tied to a lost context, and which must be reinvented when the environment, population, and future are different?

This theme is strongest when expressed through:
- continuity versus adaptation;
- memory versus lived reality;
- efficiency versus resilience;
- inheritance versus responsibility;
- restoration versus transformation.

**CONSTRAINT**

The theme must not become:
- a morality meter;
- a mandatory binary faction choice;
- a final A/B/C popup;
- a single canonical “correct civilization” answer;
- a requirement that sandbox systems be subordinated to a linear narrative ending.

# 2. HUMANITY FOUNDATION

## 2.1 What is known about humanity

**CONFIRMED**
- Humanity suffered a planetary catastrophe.
- Only a fraction of humanity remains within the currently established product framing.
- A small group reaches a new planet and begins without established local infrastructure.
- Rebuilding settlement capability is a core product goal.

**PO DECISION**
- The mission is framed as Seed Colony / Civilization Recovery.
- The colony does not know with certainty whether other human communities survived elsewhere.

## 2.2 The Seed Colony as an inheritance problem

**PROPOSAL**

The Seed Colony should be understood narratively as carrying fragments of a civilization, not a perfect copy of one.

It brings some combination of:
- technical knowledge;
- institutional memory;
- cultural memory;
- historical records;
- practical doctrine;
- assumptions about what a functioning civilization should look like.

The important narrative property is not exactly what archive format exists. The important property is that inherited knowledge is **partial context**.

A blueprint can describe how something was built without proving that rebuilding it exactly is wise.

A historical record can preserve what people believed without proving that those beliefs fit the colony’s new conditions.

A mission doctrine can preserve priorities without anticipating every reality the settlers face.

This creates thematic space for player-built civilization to feel like continuation and authorship at the same time.

## 2.3 Preservation, restoration, adaptation, reinvention

**PROPOSAL**

These four terms are recommended as **interpretive lenses**, not formal factions or gameplay systems.

### Preservation
“What knowledge, memory, and identity must not be lost?”

Narrative value:
- creates emotional continuity with humanity before the catastrophe;
- gives rebuilding meaning beyond efficiency;
- prevents reinvention from becoming amnesia.

Risk if overused:
- nostalgia becomes unquestioned authority.

### Restoration
“What capabilities must return because civilization cannot function safely without them?”

Narrative value:
- distinguishes rebuilding from primitive survival;
- makes infrastructure and institutions feel civilizational rather than merely utilitarian.

Risk if overused:
- recreates systems because they are familiar rather than because they fit the new world.

### Adaptation
“What must change because this planet, population, and historical moment are different?”

Narrative value:
- connects settlement to direct observation;
- validates field knowledge over inherited assumptions when reality contradicts old plans.

Risk if overused:
- short-term expediency may erase useful continuity.

### Reinvention
“What can humanity build differently now that previous structures are no longer inevitable?”

Narrative value:
- turns catastrophe from pure loss into responsibility;
- supports the PO theme without declaring an answer.

Risk if overused:
- novelty can become ideology rather than evidence-based change.

**CONSTRAINT**

These lenses must remain thematic and narrative unless separately authorized by Game Design. This artifact does not define ideologies, factions, reputation, policy mechanics, votes, laws, or settlement-management systems.

## 2.4 Uncertain human survival elsewhere

**PO DECISION**

The colony does not know with certainty whether other human communities survived elsewhere.

**PROPOSAL**

This uncertainty should affect tone more than exposition.

The colony’s work matters because:
- it may be one surviving branch among several;
- it may be isolated for a very long time;
- it may eventually reconnect with others;
- it cannot responsibly assume rescue, reinforcement, or external correction.

The narrative benefit is existential responsibility without requiring hopelessness.

The colony is not necessarily “the last humans.”

It is a human community that cannot plan as though anyone else will solve its future for it.

**OPEN QUESTION**
- How much evidence exists for other possible human survivors?
- Were other missions launched?
- Is communication loss technological, geographic, temporal, political, or simply uncertain?
- Does the Seed Colony expect eventual contact, merely hope for it, or consider it unlikely?

These remain unresolved in this foundation.

# 3. PLANET FOUNDATION

## 3.1 Surveyed before catastrophe, unknown in lived reality

**PO DECISION**

Humanity surveyed the planet before the catastrophe, but the available information is incomplete.

**PROPOSAL**

The cleanest narrative foundation is a three-layer “survey gap” model:

1. **Mission-limited survey** — pre-catastrophe surveys were designed to answer settlement-relevant questions, not necessarily archaeological or civilizational ones.
2. **Archive incompleteness** — not all context, raw data, annotations, revisions, or institutional knowledge survived intact into the Seed Colony mission.
3. **Context gap** — remote or historical survey data cannot substitute for direct presence on a world whose ecology, terrain, and hidden structures are now being encountered at human scale.

This model explains why the settlers are neither ignorant nor omniscient.

They can arrive with expectations while still discovering genuinely new truths.

**CONSTRAINT**

This document does not define:
- specific sensor technologies;
- data formats;
- orbital platforms;
- map-resolution mechanics;
- procedural generation behavior;
- exact archive-loss causes.

## 3.2 Expected planet versus encountered planet

**PROPOSAL**

The planet should support a recurring narrative contrast:

### What humanity expected
A place described through survey abstractions:
- terrain classifications;
- climate tendencies;
- resource probabilities;
- broad habitability judgments;
- known hazards.

### What settlement reveals
A place experienced through:
- local ecology;
- seasonal or weather behavior;
- inaccessible or hidden structures;
- relationships between geography and old infrastructure;
- evidence that appears meaningless in isolation but gains significance when compared across locations.

The point is not that the old survey was “wrong.”

The point is that a survey can be technically correct and narratively incomplete.

## 3.3 The planet as a palimpsest

**PROPOSAL**

The recommended long-term worldbuilding model is a **palimpsest**: multiple histories share the same landscape.

Potential layers include:

1. **Natural planetary history**
2. **Prior-civilization activity**
3. **Possible deeper or older anomalies**
4. **Current human settlement**
5. **Future player-authored civilization**

These layers should overlap rather than occupy isolated lore zones.

A human road can cross an older route.

A settlement can grow beside a ruin whose purpose remains uncertain.

An ecological pattern can matter simultaneously as a resource reality, a natural system, and possible evidence of older intervention.

This allows narrative to emerge from the same persistent world the player is already building in.

**OPEN QUESTION**

Whether “Something Older” is truly a separate historical layer, an earlier phase of the same civilization, a natural planetary phenomenon, or a mistaken human categorization remains unresolved.

# 4. PRIOR CIVILIZATION FOUNDATION

## 4.1 Phase 1 canon invariants

**CONFIRMED**

The following facts are preserved exactly from accepted #65 narrative canon:

1. The Phase 1 ruin is not a current human colony artifact.
2. It predates the current landing.
3. It is intentionally engineered evidence of an earlier technological presence.
4. The builders are absent from the Phase 1 encounter.
5. The site does not reveal its full purpose.
6. The builders’ identity remains unresolved.
7. The builders’ fate remains unresolved.
8. The relationship between the builders and humanity remains unresolved.
9. The Ancient Alloy Shard is physical evidence associated with the ruin but does not decode the civilization.
10. The first ruin creates a continuing mystery rather than solving one.

## 4.2 What the World Bible may develop

**PROPOSAL**

Future prior-civilization worldbuilding is strongest if it focuses first on **patterns of behavior** rather than names, species, biographies, or exposition.

Useful questions include:
- Did they maintain what they built?
- Did they repair or replace?
- Did they centralize or distribute infrastructure?
- Did all regions behave the same way?
- Were sites abandoned, dismantled, repurposed, or transformed?
- What relationships existed between infrastructure and ecology?
- What did they preserve?
- What did they intentionally remove?
- What remains where removal should have occurred?
- What is absent where remains would normally be expected?

These questions allow a civilization to become legible through consequences before its identity is known.

## 4.3 A civilization should not read as a single personality

**PROPOSAL**

The prior civilization should eventually feel historically plural rather than like one unified “ancient race.”

Evidence may suggest:
- different periods;
- changing standards;
- different regional practices;
- competing solutions;
- disagreement;
- maintenance versus abandonment;
- reuse of old systems;
- later modifications over earlier structures.

This does not require formal factions.

It only prevents the world from implying that every builder across all history shared one intention.

**CONSTRAINT**

No faction, government, species, religion, ideology, war, or political structure is canonized by this proposal.

## 4.4 The Great Dispersal

**PROPOSAL**

“Great Dispersal” is recommended as a **working editorial label for a mystery hypothesis**, not an established historical fact or necessarily an in-world term.

The hypothesis is that a late historical pattern may initially look like collapse or evacuation, but later evidence could suggest:
- deliberate decentralization;
- distributed relocation;
- systemic dismantling;
- transformation of how the civilization occupied space;
- a change in what counted as a “site,” “settlement,” or “presence.”

**OPEN QUESTION**

The Great Dispersal may ultimately be:
- physical migration;
- technological transition;
- social decentralization;
- ecological integration;
- deliberate shutdown;
- partial evacuation;
- multiple unrelated processes incorrectly grouped together;
- something else.

**CONSTRAINT**

The final nature, cause, chronology, and outcome of the Great Dispersal must remain unconfirmed until Project Owner approval.

## 4.5 Something Older

**PROPOSAL**

“Something Older” is recommended as a **working interpretive category**, not the name of an entity, species, god, faction, intelligence, or confirmed civilization.

It refers to evidence that appears difficult to fit cleanly into the working model of the known prior civilization.

Possible evidence categories may include:
- incompatible construction layers;
- reused foundations;
- anomalous alignments beneath later structures;
- ecological patterns older than nearby engineered remains;
- materials or modification styles that do not match the current comparison set.

**OPEN QUESTION**

“Something Older” may ultimately be:
- an earlier phase of the same civilization;
- a different civilization;
- a natural process mistaken for engineering;
- an inherited technology;
- a planetary-scale phenomenon;
- a category error caused by insufficient evidence.

**CONSTRAINT**

No supernatural, cosmic-horror, creator-species, precursor-of-the-precursors, or final-metaphysical explanation is established here.

# 5. CORE THEME

## 5.1 The theme as a recurring question, not an answer

**PO DECISION**

Core theme:

> When given the chance to build civilization again from the beginning, should humanity restore the old civilization or build something better?

**PROPOSAL**

The narrative should repeatedly complicate this question rather than resolve it through a single speech or ending.

The prior civilization can mirror the theme by showing that:
- maintenance is a choice;
- preservation is selective;
- infrastructure encodes assumptions;
- adaptation has costs;
- continuity can require change;
- abandoning a system is not always equivalent to failure;
- rebuilding a familiar system can reproduce familiar vulnerabilities.

These are thematic possibilities, not claims about the prior civilization’s final history.

## 5.2 Five thematic mirrors

**PROPOSAL**

### Infrastructure
Question:
What does a civilization choose to make permanent?

Human mirror:
The colony turns temporary survival into durable settlement.

Prior-civilization mirror:
Old systems reveal what earlier builders expected to endure.

### Ecology
Question:
Does civilization exist apart from its environment or inside it?

Human mirror:
Settlement changes the planet and is changed by it.

Prior-civilization mirror:
Environmental patterns may preserve traces of long-term choices.

### Memory
Question:
What survives when institutions and people do not?

Human mirror:
The Seed Colony inherits partial records and cultural memory.

Prior-civilization mirror:
Humanity reconstructs another civilization from consequences rather than complete testimony.

### Inheritance
Question:
Is receiving a system the same as consenting to it?

Human mirror:
Settlers inherit pre-catastrophe knowledge and expectations.

Prior-civilization mirror:
Later sites may reuse, modify, reject, or dismantle earlier systems.

### Transformation
Question:
When does continuity require becoming different?

Human mirror:
Civilization Recovery may produce a civilization unlike the one that was lost.

Prior-civilization mirror:
The Great Dispersal hypothesis may eventually complicate the assumption that disappearance equals destruction.

## 5.3 Thematic rule

**CONSTRAINT**

The prior civilization must not become a simple cautionary tale whose only function is “they made mistake X; therefore humanity must choose Y.”

The mystery should offer evidence with enough ambiguity that players can form different legitimate provisional interpretations.

The player’s civilization remains authored through sandbox play, not judged by a hidden narrative score.

# 6. SANDBOX NARRATIVE PHILOSOPHY

## 6.1 Narrative supports the sandbox

**PO DECISION**
- No mandatory fixed main-quest line.
- No fixed ruin order required for story comprehension.
- No mandatory boss required to continue narrative understanding.
- No narrative gate that blocks core sandbox progression.
- No game-over after central mystery understanding.
- No mandatory final A/B/C choice.
- The sandbox continues after major narrative understanding.
- BUILD CIVILIZATION remains the core player fantasy.

## 6.2 Narrative as an optional deepening layer

**PROPOSAL**

The main mystery should function as a layer of meaning that can be engaged with deeply without making ordinary settlement play feel like “wrong play.”

A player who explores extensively may build a rich theory of the planet.

A player who focuses on settlement should still inhabit a world whose history is visible around them.

Narrative therefore should:
- reward observation;
- accumulate context;
- reframe familiar places;
- create reasons to revisit;
- remain compatible with different player pacing.

It should not require every player to consume the same exposition in the same order.

## 6.3 Acts are epistemic states, not mandatory chapters

**PROPOSAL**

The six-act working structure from Issue #82 is best treated as **epistemic maturity**, not chronological mission order.

A player may encounter evidence associated with a later interpretive layer before fully understanding an earlier one.

The “act” indicates the kind of theory the evidence makes possible, not a locked quest stage.

This preserves nonlinear discovery.

# 7. NARRATIVE LAYERS

## Layer A — Humanity Rebuilding

**CONFIRMED / PO DECISION**

Humanity is reconstructing settlement and civilizational capability after catastrophe.

Narrative function:
- anchors stakes in what players build;
- ensures the mystery never eclipses the colony;
- makes the future as important as the past.

## Layer B — Survey Expectation versus Direct Reality

**PO DECISION / PROPOSAL**

The planet was surveyed before catastrophe, but lived settlement reveals context the survey did not preserve or prioritize.

Narrative function:
- creates credible surprise without making humanity foolish;
- establishes that maps/data and reality can both be true at different levels.

## Layer C — Prior Civilization

**CONFIRMED / PROPOSAL**

An earlier technological presence existed.

Narrative function:
- drives the main planetary mystery;
- offers historical parallels to humanity’s reconstruction;
- produces evidence that can support multiple provisional theories.

## Layer D — Great Dispersal Hypothesis

**PROPOSAL**

Patterns once read as collapse may eventually appear deliberate, selective, or transformative.

Narrative function:
- converts “what killed them?” into a more interesting “what did they choose, and why?”
- challenges the assumption that absence equals failure.

## Layer E — Something Older Hypothesis

**PROPOSAL**

Some evidence may resist classification within the current model of the prior civilization.

Narrative function:
- keeps the world larger than the first solved mystery;
- prevents the prior civilization from becoming the final explanatory layer.

## Layer F — Humanity as the Next Historical Layer

**PO DECISION / PROPOSAL**

Human settlement becomes part of the same planet’s history.

Narrative function:
- closes the thematic loop without ending the game;
- reframes every player-built structure as future inheritance;
- asks what traces humanity itself will leave.

# 8. MYSTERY ARCHITECTURE

## 8.1 Working central structure

### ACT I — Someone Was Here

**CONFIRMED**

Phase 1 establishes intentional earlier technological presence.

Knowledge state:
“Someone built here before us.”

### ACT II — Something Happened

**PROPOSAL**

As evidence accumulates, the simplest theory trends toward disappearance, collapse, or abandonment.

Knowledge state:
“This civilization is absent, and its absence has a history.”

### ACT III — They Chose This

**PROPOSAL**

Repeated patterns of removal, shutdown, maintenance, or controlled dismantling complicate pure-collapse theories.

Knowledge state:
“Some of what looks like loss may have been intentional.”

### ACT IV — They Were Becoming Something Else

**PROPOSAL**

The Great Dispersal hypothesis reframes disappearance as possible decentralization, transformation, or a change in how the civilization occupied the planet.

Knowledge state:
“Absence from familiar sites may not equal extinction.”

### ACT V — They Were Not First

**PROPOSAL**

Some evidence appears older, incompatible, or inherited.

Knowledge state:
“Our model of the prior civilization may itself be incomplete.”

### ACT VI — We Are Next

**PO DECISION / PROPOSAL**

Humanity recognizes that its new civilization is becoming another historical layer of the planet.

Knowledge state:
“The question is no longer only what happened before us, but what we are building now.”

**CONSTRAINT**

Act VI is not an ending.

No Act requires a fixed ruin order, mandatory boss, final cutscene, or hard narrative gate.

# 8.2 NONLINEAR MYSTERY THREADS

All seven threads below are **PROPOSAL** structures for creative review. None automatically establishes the truth of the interpretations it contains.

## Thread 1 — Absence

**PROPOSAL**

### Core question
Why is engineered infrastructure present without its builders?

### Evidence categories
- functional-looking spaces without occupants;
- absence of expected habitation traces;
- sites that appear maintained longer than expected;
- shutdown states that do not resemble uncontrolled destruction;
- missing categories of objects where their absence is meaningful.

### What can be learned early
The civilization is not simply “standing here waiting to be met.”

### Plausible interpretations
- catastrophe;
- evacuation;
- demographic decline;
- regional abandonment;
- deliberate withdrawal;
- changed mode of habitation.

### Intentionally withheld
- whether the builders are extinct;
- whether they left the planet;
- whether they still exist in another form;
- whether “absence” is global.

### Intersections
- Dismantling can explain missing components.
- Infrastructure Network can imply continued system behavior after local abandonment.
- Departure / Transformation can reframe physical absence.
- Internal Disagreement can reveal that absence was not uniform.

### Later recontextualization
A site first read as “abandoned” may later appear deliberately closed, emptied, or converted.

### What remains open
Even substantial evidence may not prove the builders’ final fate.

### Order-independence rule
Absence evidence must be observable without requiring a prior explanation. Later threads change interpretation, not the physical fact that users are absent.

## Thread 2 — Dismantling

**PROPOSAL**

### Core question
Why are important parts missing in repeated, patterned ways?

### Evidence categories
- clean removal boundaries;
- repeated absence of the same component class;
- disassembly traces;
- recovered/reused structural interfaces;
- sites where removal appears safer or more orderly than collapse.

### What can be learned early
Some missing material may have been removed rather than destroyed.

### Plausible interpretations
- salvage during scarcity;
- evacuation;
- planned decommissioning;
- resource consolidation;
- technological transition;
- ritualized or policy-driven removal.

### Intentionally withheld
- who ordered removal;
- whether removal happened everywhere;
- where removed components went;
- whether dismantling was crisis behavior or normal lifecycle practice.

### Intersections
- Internal Disagreement can show different removal patterns.
- Infrastructure Network can reveal whether removed parts were redistributed.
- Departure / Transformation can interpret dismantling as preparation.
- Something Older can reveal reuse of older foundations.

### Later recontextualization
What first looks like desperate scavenging may later look like coordinated decommissioning.

### What remains open
Intentional removal still does not prove the ultimate destination or fate of the builders.

### Order-independence rule
The physical fact is “component absent with evidence of controlled removal,” not “they were preparing to leave.” The latter remains interpretation.

## Thread 3 — Infrastructure Network

**PROPOSAL**

### Core question
Were individual sites independent, or parts of a larger distributed system?

### Evidence categories
- repeated interfaces;
- alignments across distance;
- consistent connection geometries;
- structures whose local purpose makes more sense when compared with others;
- infrastructure that appears overbuilt for a single site;
- recurring network-like topology.

### What can be learned early
Some ruins may have belonged to systems larger than themselves.

### Plausible interpretations
- transport;
- power;
- environmental regulation;
- communication;
- logistics;
- distributed computation;
- functions humans do not yet recognize.

### Intentionally withheld
- exact network purpose;
- whether all observed networks served one function;
- whether the network was civilization-wide;
- whether it remains active.

### Intersections
- Ecology can suggest environmental coupling.
- Dismantling can show selective removal from networks.
- Departure / Transformation can suggest decentralization.
- Something Older can reveal infrastructure built on inherited alignments.

### Later recontextualization
A “facility” may later be understood as a junction, relay, boundary, or support element within a system.

### What remains open
Understanding topology does not automatically reveal cultural meaning or builder identity.

### Order-independence rule
Each node/site must remain interpretable locally; cross-site comparison adds context rather than unlocking the only valid explanation.

## Thread 4 — Ecology

**PROPOSAL**

### Core question
Which ecological patterns are natural, which are influenced by past activity, and which only appear connected?

### Evidence categories
- unusual species distribution near old structures;
- environmental boundaries correlated with infrastructure;
- long-lived altered habitats;
- recovery zones;
- mismatches between expected and observed ecology;
- ecological patterns that predate recent human activity.

### What can be learned early
The planet’s present ecology may carry historical information.

### Plausible interpretations
- deliberate stewardship;
- accidental contamination;
- terraforming;
- maintenance ecology;
- natural adaptation to abandoned structures;
- coincidence;
- inherited effects from something older.

### Intentionally withheld
- whether the prior civilization engineered the ecology;
- whether all anomalies share one cause;
- whether ecological influence was beneficial or harmful;
- whether observed patterns were intentional.

### Intersections
- Infrastructure Network can imply coupled systems.
- Something Older can complicate ownership of anomalies.
- Internal Disagreement can show incompatible environmental interventions.
- Humanity Rebuilding creates a thematic mirror as human settlement also changes the environment.

### Later recontextualization
A “natural anomaly” may become evidence of long-term interaction; an assumed engineered zone may later prove partly natural.

### What remains open
Causation may remain uncertain even when correlation is strong.

### Order-independence rule
Evidence must preserve the distinction between correlation and proven intent.

## Thread 5 — Internal Disagreement

**PROPOSAL**

### Core question
Did the prior civilization share one plan, or do sites preserve evidence of competing choices?

### Evidence categories
- incompatible modifications;
- repairs that reverse earlier changes;
- regional standards that cannot be explained solely by age;
- partial shutdowns beside maintained systems;
- reused structures serving different later purposes;
- conflicting treatment of the same infrastructure type.

### What can be learned early
The historical record may not represent a single unified intention.

### Plausible interpretations
- different eras;
- regional adaptation;
- technical evolution;
- institutional disagreement;
- cultural disagreement;
- emergency improvisation.

### Intentionally withheld
- formal factions;
- ideology;
- names;
- political structure;
- whether disagreement became conflict.

### Intersections
- Dismantling can show different responses to the same system.
- Departure / Transformation can reveal nonuniform participation.
- Ecology can show different intervention philosophies.
- Infrastructure Network can show local deviations from shared standards.

### Later recontextualization
What first appears to be design inconsistency may become evidence of historical debate or divergent choices.

### What remains open
Evidence of disagreement does not prove war, schism, rebellion, or violence.

### Order-independence rule
Different modifications must be factual layers; the “disagreement” interpretation remains provisional until comparisons make it plausible.

## Thread 6 — Departure / Transformation

**PROPOSAL**

### Core question
Did the civilization disappear, leave, disperse, or change what presence meant?

### Evidence categories
- deliberate shutdown;
- staged dismantling;
- infrastructure that remains coherent after local abandonment;
- missing expected remains;
- redistribution patterns;
- signs of transition rather than abrupt destruction.

### What can be learned early
Simple extinction/collapse may not explain all evidence.

### Plausible interpretations
- evacuation;
- migration;
- decentralization;
- social transformation;
- technological transformation;
- reduced physical infrastructure;
- regional retreat;
- incomplete or uneven transition.

### Intentionally withheld
- destination;
- survival status;
- form of transformation;
- cause;
- whether transition succeeded.

### Intersections
- Absence establishes the initial mystery.
- Dismantling suggests preparation.
- Infrastructure Network may support distributed presence.
- Internal Disagreement may show that not everyone made the same choice.
- Something Older may have influenced the transition—or may be unrelated.

### Later recontextualization
The Great Dispersal hypothesis can transform “they vanished” into “they may have deliberately ceased occupying the world in the way humans expected.”

### What remains open
The final fate of the civilization remains unresolved until explicitly approved.

### Order-independence rule
Players can encounter transformation-compatible evidence early, but it should not announce the hypothesis as fact without a broader evidence pattern.

## Thread 7 — Something Older

**PROPOSAL**

### Core question
Does all deep-history evidence belong to the civilization humanity is currently studying?

### Evidence categories
- incompatible construction layers;
- anomalous foundations;
- older material relationships;
- infrastructure reuse;
- features whose erosion/context suggests different chronology;
- ecological or geological relationships that resist the current model.

### What can be learned early
Some evidence does not fit comfortably.

### Plausible interpretations
- earlier phase of the same civilization;
- predecessor culture;
- inherited technology;
- natural formation;
- mistaken dating;
- unrelated planetary process.

### Intentionally withheld
- whether a separate intelligence existed;
- exact age;
- relationship to the known prior civilization;
- whether “older” means chronologically older or only stylistically different.

### Intersections
- Infrastructure Network may reuse older alignments.
- Ecology may preserve deeper effects.
- Internal Disagreement may actually be chronology confusion.
- Departure / Transformation may have been shaped by older discoveries, or not.

### Later recontextualization
A layer initially assigned to the known builders may later be recognized as inherited, reused, or misclassified.

### What remains open
“Something Older” must remain a question until evidence justifies a narrower interpretation.

### Order-independence rule
Anomalies should be observable before they are explainable. They must not require a secret codex entry to become “real.”

# 9. REVEAL / RECONTEXTUALIZATION RULES

## 9.1 Required model

**PO DECISION**

Narrative discovery follows:

> EVIDENCE → INTERPRETATION → CONTRADICTION → RECONTEXTUALIZATION

**CONSTRAINT**
- Evidence does not intentionally lie.
- Player interpretation may be incomplete.
- Later discoveries should challenge assumptions rather than retcon observed facts.
- Direct exposition should not replace environmental inference where the environment can carry the idea.

## 9.2 Authoring distinction

**PROPOSAL**

Every major reveal should separate four layers:

1. **Observed Fact** — what is physically or relationally supported.
2. **Natural Interpretation** — the explanation a reasonable observer might first prefer.
3. **Alternative Interpretation** — another explanation already compatible with the evidence.
4. **Later Recontextualization** — evidence that changes which interpretation is strongest without erasing the observed fact.

## 9.3 Major discovery pattern A — Empty but orderly

**PROPOSAL**

**Observed Fact:**  
A site lacks users and expected active habitation traces, yet key areas show controlled shutdown or preserved organization.

**Natural Interpretation:**  
The builders abandoned the site during a crisis.

**Alternative Interpretation:**  
The site was deliberately decommissioned.

**Later Recontextualization:**  
Comparable sites show repeated shutdown order, suggesting a pattern rather than one emergency.

Narrative purpose:
Move Absence toward Dismantling/Departure without proving the Great Dispersal.

## 9.4 Major discovery pattern B — Missing components

**PROPOSAL**

**Observed Fact:**  
Specific components are absent with repeatable removal traces.

**Natural Interpretation:**  
Survivors scavenged useful material after collapse.

**Alternative Interpretation:**  
The builders themselves removed components before leaving the site.

**Later Recontextualization:**  
Other sites contain matching receiver interfaces or redistribution evidence.

Narrative purpose:
Reframe scarcity/scavenging as possible planned relocation.

## 9.5 Major discovery pattern C — The site is not the system

**PROPOSAL**

**Observed Fact:**  
Structures share orientation, interfaces, or connection logic across distance.

**Natural Interpretation:**  
They are multiple examples of the same building type.

**Alternative Interpretation:**  
They are different nodes in one distributed network.

**Later Recontextualization:**  
A locally meaningless structure becomes legible only when mapped relationally against others.

Narrative purpose:
Shift player thinking from isolated ruins to planetary systems.

## 9.6 Major discovery pattern D — Ecological anomaly

**PROPOSAL**

**Observed Fact:**  
An ecological pattern repeatedly correlates with old infrastructure.

**Natural Interpretation:**  
The abandoned structure contaminated or altered the environment.

**Alternative Interpretation:**  
The infrastructure may have been built in response to an existing ecological condition.

**Later Recontextualization:**  
Older anomalies appear where known infrastructure does not, weakening a simple “builders caused everything” theory.

Narrative purpose:
Keep causation open and create a bridge toward Something Older.

## 9.7 Major discovery pattern E — Conflicting modifications

**PROPOSAL**

**Observed Fact:**  
Later alterations reverse or bypass earlier design choices.

**Natural Interpretation:**  
The builders improved obsolete technology.

**Alternative Interpretation:**  
Different communities or periods disagreed about how systems should operate.

**Later Recontextualization:**  
Regional clusters show different long-term modification patterns.

Narrative purpose:
Make the civilization feel historically plural without canonizing factions.

## 9.8 Major discovery pattern F — Disappearance without catastrophe

**PROPOSAL**

**Observed Fact:**  
Some sites lack the damage, remains, or disorder expected from sudden destruction.

**Natural Interpretation:**  
Occupants evacuated before catastrophe reached them.

**Alternative Interpretation:**  
There may have been no catastrophe at those sites.

**Later Recontextualization:**  
Shutdown and dismantling sequences appear coordinated across distant areas.

Narrative purpose:
Challenge the assumption that civilization absence must equal civilizational failure.

## 9.9 Major discovery pattern G — Incompatible deep layer

**PROPOSAL**

**Observed Fact:**  
A structural or environmental layer does not match the current comparison set for known prior-civilization works.

**Natural Interpretation:**  
It is an unusually old or damaged example of the same tradition.

**Alternative Interpretation:**  
It belongs to an earlier phase, inherited system, or unrelated process.

**Later Recontextualization:**  
Additional mismatched layers form a pattern that the existing model cannot explain.

Narrative purpose:
Open Something Older without declaring a second civilization.

# 10. ENVIRONMENTAL STORYTELLING RULES

## 10.1 Physical evidence before explanation

**CONSTRAINT**

When an idea can be communicated through persistent environmental evidence, that evidence should carry the first burden of proof.

Logs, voice, text, or direct exposition may later clarify context, but should not be the only reason the world’s central mystery makes sense.

## 10.2 Site condition is historical evidence

**PROPOSAL**

Condition should imply process, not merely age.

Useful distinctions include:
- collapse;
- erosion;
- clean removal;
- emergency repair;
- routine repair;
- abandonment;
- deliberate shutdown;
- reuse;
- partial maintenance.

The narrative question is not “does this look old?” but “what happened to it over time?”

**CONSTRAINT**

Exact visual expression remains Art authority.

## 10.3 Negative space must be meaningful

**PROPOSAL**

Absence becomes evidence only when there is a reason to expect something.

Examples:
- an interface with no attached component;
- a maintained route that leads to a removed node;
- repeated empty anchor points;
- infrastructure sized for a missing counterpart.

An empty room by itself is not a mystery.

## 10.4 Dismantling must differ from destruction

**PROPOSAL**

When the narrative needs players to consider deliberate removal, evidence should support a distinction between:
- broken;
- decayed;
- removed;
- repurposed.

The distinction can be partial and uncertain, but should not depend on arbitrary text telling the player what happened.

## 10.5 Topology carries meaning

**PROPOSAL**

Infrastructure relationships should matter narratively.

A player comparing multiple sites should be able to notice:
- repeated alignment;
- network shape;
- directionality;
- hierarchy;
- redundancy;
- missing links.

This does not require a specific map system or gameplay mechanic.

## 10.6 Ecology can preserve history

**PROPOSAL**

Environmental storytelling may treat living systems as historical records.

Possibilities include:
- species that cluster around old infrastructure;
- zones of unexpected recovery;
- ecological boundaries that align with engineered ones;
- habitats formed by abandonment;
- evidence that natural systems outlived technological systems.

**CONSTRAINT**

This does not canonize terraforming, ecological engineering, or specific biological mechanisms.

## 10.7 Layering is more valuable than isolated spectacle

**PROPOSAL**

A strong mystery site can contain multiple historical layers:
- original construction;
- later modification;
- repair;
- removal;
- natural reclamation;
- human interpretation.

Layering lets the same place support new meaning after later discoveries.

## 10.8 Repeated motifs should signal relationship before meaning

**PROPOSAL**

Recurring shapes, patterns, interfaces, or arrangements may tell players “these things are related” long before anyone knows what they mean.

**CONSTRAINT**

A repeated motif must not be silently promoted to a translated word, religious symbol, faction emblem, or language unit without later approval.

## 10.9 Disagreement should appear as incompatible choices

**PROPOSAL**

Evidence of internal difference is stronger when expressed as:
- one site preserving what another removes;
- one region maintaining what another bypasses;
- later work undoing earlier work;
- different treatment of the same inherited system.

This avoids the shortcut of inventing named factions before the evidence supports them.

## 10.10 Evidence comparison is the engine of understanding

**PROPOSAL**

Single clues should usually establish facts.

Comparison between clues should usually establish interpretations.

This creates nonlinear robustness because players can encounter facts in many orders and still form theories from relationships among them.

## 10.11 No “secret truth object”

**CONSTRAINT**

The central mystery should not depend on one final log, terminal, relic, cutscene, boss, or NPC whose discovery instantly overrides all prior inference.

A later direct statement may confirm part of the picture, but it should fit evidence the world has already made visible.

## Humanity
- What exactly caused the human catastrophe?
- How much of pre-catastrophe civilization is culturally remembered versus technically archived?
- Were multiple Seed Colony / Civilization Recovery efforts attempted?

- Who authorized or designed the mission?
- Was it planned before catastrophe, improvised during it, or adapted from another program?
- What did mission planners believe the planet would be like?
- What mission assumptions prove most incomplete after settlement?

- When was the pre-catastrophe survey conducted?
- How much survey data survives?
- Which gaps are due to survey purpose versus later data loss?
- Were prior-civilization traces noticed, misclassified, inaccessible, or absent from the surviving dataset?

## Prior civilization
- Who were the builders?
- Were they a single civilization across all relevant sites?
- How long did they occupy the planet?
- What did they call themselves?
- What did they value?
- Why are they absent?
- What was the role of their infrastructure?
- Did they intentionally alter the ecology?
- Were observed differences chronological, regional, ideological, technical, or all of the above?

## Great Dispersal
- Did it actually occur as one coherent historical process?
- Was it migration, decentralization, transformation, dismantling, or a human interpretive label for multiple processes?
- Was participation voluntary or uneven?
- Was it a response to crisis or a long-planned transition?
- Did it succeed?

## Something Older
- Is it real as a distinct historical category?
- Is it artificial?
- Is it connected to the prior civilization?
- Did the prior civilization understand it?
- Did it influence the Great Dispersal?
- Is the phrase itself useful enough to retain after review?

## Central mystery
- What level of understanding counts as “substantially understood” while still leaving the world open?
- Which truths should remain unresolved even after long-term narrative progress?
- How much direct testimony should ever exist compared with environmental inference?
